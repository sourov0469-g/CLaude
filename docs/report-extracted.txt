[Heading1] Executive Summary
[] This report looks at how PizzaBurg manages its people. PizzaBurg is a local pizza and burger chain. It started with one shop in Mirpur in January 2018 and now runs 22 outlets around the country.
[] We got our information from one source above all. On 26 August 2026 we sat down with Mr. Ranian Datta, the General Manager of Human Resource, and asked him how the company handles its workers. Everything in Chapter 5 comes from that meeting.
[] He told us eight main things. PizzaBurg runs 22 outlets and gives each outlet a fixed number of workers. It does not raise or lower that number based on how busy a shop is. Turnover among office staff is very low. Turnover among floor and kitchen staff is medium. Every worker must give two months notice before leaving. New kitchen staff are hired in a one day process where the company looks at their past work and often asks them to cook something before deciding. Workers get help with housing and food, plus health checkups and maternity care. Performance is checked every month, and what the check finds is used to decide who needs training. A worker can be fired for breaking company rules, for sexual harassment, for dishonesty and for other serious misconduct.
[] Put together, these practices point one way. PizzaBurg builds its people system around keeping 22 kitchens open and running. That is the goal behind almost every rule the manager described.
[] Some of what the company does is genuinely good. Asking a cook to actually cook before you hire him is a much better test than reading his certificate. Checking performance monthly is better than the once a year check most companies use. Housing and food help are worth more to a low paid worker than a small pay rise would be. And the two month notice rule matches Section 27 of the Bangladesh Labour Act 2006 exactly, which asks a permanent worker for 60 days notice.
[] The weak spots are all of one type. The company does things but does not write them down. There is no scoring sheet for the cooking test. No fixed list of things the monthly check measures. No record of who left and why. No written steps to follow before firing someone.
[] Two of those gaps carry real risk. Section 24 of the Labour Act says a worker cannot be punished unless the charge is put in writing, the worker gets at least seven days to reply, he gets a hearing, and a joint committee looks into it. Nothing like that was described to us. Also, PizzaBurg names sexual harassment as a firing offence but has no complaint committee. A 2009 High Court order says every workplace in the country must have one. So the company has a rule with no proper place to apply it.
[] We end with ten suggestions. The first two cost almost nothing and should be done first. Set up the harassment complaint committee. Write down the steps for handling misconduct. After that, a scoring sheet for the cooking test, a proper monthly review form, and counting turnover instead of just calling it medium.
[] One warning about all of this. We spoke to one person for one meeting. We did not see any company files and we did not talk to any workers. So when we say something is missing, we mean nobody told us about it. It may still exist.

[Heading2] 2.6  The Food Business in Bangladesh

The workers are young, which makes them easy to train but harder to keep. And the industry is growing fast, so everyone is fighting for the same experienced staff. Against that background, a company reporting only medium turnover among floor staff is doing reasonably well.
[] Chapter Three
[Heading1] What Human Resource Management Means
[Heading2] 3.1  The Basic Idea
[] Human resource management is the set of systems a company uses to find workers, put them in the right jobs, train them, pay them, judge their work, look after them, control their behaviour and handle them when they leave.
[] Two words in that sentence matter most. The first is systems. These jobs are connected. Hire carelessly and you pay for it later in training. Find a weakness with no training to fix it and the check was pointless. Fire someone with no paperwork and you lose the case. So you cannot judge one practice alone.
[] The second is written. A shop owner who knows all six of his workers can run things by memory. A company with a thousand workers in 22 towns cannot. At that size judgement has to be turned into forms and rules or it stops being the same judgement from shop to shop. Most of what we say in Chapter 5 comes back to this point.
[Heading2] 3.2  The Main Jobs of HRM
[] Table 3.1  What each HR function does
=== TABLE ===
Function | What it is for
Manpower planning | Works out how many workers are needed, with what skills, and where.
Recruitment | Brings in people to choose from.
Selection | Picks the right person out of that group.
Induction | Settles the new worker into the job quickly.
Training | Builds the skills the worker needs now and later.
Performance management | Sets what is expected, watches it, judges it and gives feedback.
Pay and benefits | Decides what the worker gets in return for the work.
Welfare | Support beyond wages that makes working life easier.
Discipline | Handles behaviour, complaints and punishment.
Separation | Manages resignation, retirement and firing.
=== END TABLE ===
[] Source: prepared by us from standard HRM textbooks.
[Heading2] 3.3  Planning How Many Workers You Need
[] There are two common ways to do this.
[] One way is to change the number of workers with demand. Put more people on during the evening rush and fewer at quiet times. This saves money because nobody stands idle. But it is hard to organise, it makes training messy, and workers never know how much they will earn.
[] The other way is to fix a number for each shop and keep it there. This is simple, the cost is easy to budget, the team stays together and workers know their hours. The cost is that some hours will be slow with too many staff and some rushes will be short of hands.
[] Neither is better in general. PizzaBurg uses the second way. We look at that choice in section 5.1.
[Heading2] 3.4  Choosing the Right Person
[] Hiring has two parts. Recruitment brings people in. Selection chooses among them. A method is judged on whether it predicts real job performance, whether it gives the same answer when different people use it, and whether the company can afford the time.
[] Research on hiring keeps finding the same thing for skilled hands on jobs. Watching someone do a real task from the job predicts performance far better than an ordinary interview or a look at their certificates. This matters here because PizzaBurg asks cooks to cook. But the same research adds a condition. The test only gives the same answer twice if the person watching is marking against a fixed list. If two managers judge by feeling, they will pick differently. We return to this in section 5.2.
[Heading2] 3.5  Why Workers Leave, and Training
[] Turnover is counted two ways. Voluntary means the worker quit. Involuntary means the company let him go. Separately, useful turnover means a weak worker left. Harmful turnover means a good one did. A single number mixing all four tells you very little. Replacing a worker costs money in paperwork, in hiring time, in training, and in output lost while the seat is empty. In a restaurant the last is the biggest and the hardest to see, because it shows up as slow service and not as a figure in the accounts.
[] A notice period does not stop people leaving. It only turns a sudden loss into a planned one. Its worth depends on how hard the person is to replace and on what the company does with the time.
[] Training should start with finding the gap. Many companies skip that and buy training because it was available or because a budget had to be spent. That kind of training fixes problems nobody had. Checking performance is a separate job. Once a year is cheap, but the feedback arrives too late to fix anything. Checking often catches problems early but eats manager time.
[] What decides whether a check is useful is not how often it happens. It is whether the things being judged are clear and related to the job, whether the important ones carry more weight, and whether the result is written down and used.
[Heading2] 3.6  Welfare and What Motivates People
[] Welfare means what the company gives beyond wages. Some of it is required by law. Some is given by choice.
[] Two old ideas explain why welfare works. The first says people meet basic needs first, food and shelter and safety, before anything higher matters. So housing and food help hit exactly where a low paid worker feels it. The second splits benefits in two. Some only stop people being unhappy. Others make people want to work harder. Housing and meals are the first kind. They remove complaints and make leaving costly, but they do not create drive. Drive comes from being noticed and promoted. We come back to this in section 5.5.
[Heading2] 3.7  Discipline
[] There are two ways to handle discipline. One is to punish the offence to match how serious it is, up to dismissal. The other is to warn in steps. A talk, then a written warning, then a final warning, then suspension, and dismissal last. The second is better for problems a worker can correct. It keeps someone the company already trained and it leaves a paper trail. It is not right for theft or violence or serious harassment, which call for dismissal after a proper inquiry.
[] Either way, some basic fairness rules apply. Tell the worker exactly what he is accused of. Let him answer before deciding. Have someone neutral decide, not the person complaining. Write it all down. In Bangladesh these are not just good manners. They are written into the law, as the next section shows.
[Heading2] 3.8  What the Law Says
[] Any study of HR practice in Bangladesh has to be checked against the Bangladesh Labour Act 2006, along with the Labour Rules 2015 and later changes. Restaurants come under this law.
[] Table 3.2  Parts of the Labour Act 2006 that matter here
=== TABLE ===
Section | What it says
Section 5 | No worker may be employed without an appointment letter and an identity card with a photo.
Section 23 | A worker can be dismissed without notice for a criminal conviction or for misconduct. Section 23(4) lists what counts as misconduct, including wilful disobedience, theft, fraud, habitual absence and disorderly behaviour. Section 23(2) allows lighter punishments instead, such as demotion, stopping an increment, a fine, suspension or a warning.
Section 24 | No punishment under Section 23 is valid unless the charge is written down, a copy is given to the worker with at least seven days to reply, the worker is heard, an inquiry committee with equal numbers from both sides finishes within 60 days, and the employer approves the order. A worker under charge may be suspended for up to 60 days and must be paid a subsistence allowance.
Section 26 | The employer can end a permanent worker’s service without giving a reason, but only with 120 days notice if he is paid monthly or 60 days otherwise, or wages in place of notice, plus 30 days wages for each completed year of service or gratuity, whichever is higher.
Section 27 | A permanent worker who wants to resign must give 60 days notice in writing. A temporary worker gives 30 days if paid monthly and 14 days otherwise.
Sections 45 to 50 | A woman worker gets maternity benefit for eight weeks before and eight weeks after delivery, if she has worked at least six months before the delivery. Section 50 protects that benefit against a dismissal issued without good cause during the protected period.
=== END TABLE ===
[] Source: prepared by us from the Bangladesh Labour Act 2006 and published legal writing. This is a student report and not legal advice.
[] One more rule sits outside the Labour Act but matters a lot here. Bangladesh has no separate law on sexual harassment at work. In 2009 the High Court Division filled that gap in the case brought by the Bangladesh National Women Lawyers Association. The court gave binding directions that apply to every workplace, government and private. Every organisation must set up a complaint committee with outside members, look into complaints within set time limits and keep them confidential. The court said these directions carry the force of law until Parliament passes a proper act. Any company that lists sexual harassment as a firing offence is expected to have that committee. We check this in sections 5.8 and 6.2.

[Heading2] 5.1  How Many Workers Each Outlet Gets
[Heading3] What he told us
[] PizzaBurg runs 22 outlets. Each outlet has a fixed number of workers. He was clear that the company does not set that number by looking at supply and demand. The number is simply fixed for each shop.
[Heading3] What it means
[] This is the second planning method from section 3.3. It is a normal choice for a chain and it brings real benefits.
[] Table 5.1  The fixed staffing rule
=== TABLE ===
Point | What the company gains | What it gives up

[] The real question is not whether a fixed number is a good idea. It is whether the number is still the right one, and whether anyone checks. A number set on opening day only stays correct while conditions stay the same. If a branch picks up a lot of delivery orders, or a rival opens next door, or a shopping centre changes its hours, the fixed number quietly becomes wrong. Nobody notices until service breaks down or money is being wasted. Mr. Datta did not mention any review of these numbers.
[] Chapter 2 makes this sharper. Twelve shops in Dhaka and the rest scattered from Feni to Rajshahi do not face the same demand or the same labour market. One number applied across all of them has to be a compromise, and a compromise fits some shops badly.
[] In our view the fixed staffing rule is a fair choice for a company that wants cost control and the same service everywhere while it expands. It gives real benefits, and workers get steady hours out of it, which is more than most of the industry offers. The problem is not the rule. It is that nothing appears to test it. Set a number once and never check it, and it will drift away from what the shop actually needs.
[Heading2] 5.2  Hiring Kitchen and Floor Staff
[Heading3] What he told us
[] Hiring for these jobs is done in one day. The company looks at what practical experience the person already has. Candidates may be asked to show what they can do before the company decides.
[Heading3] What it means
[] The one day process answers a real problem. The moment a cook quits the shop is short handed, and every extra day of interviewing is another day of slow service or overtime for the rest. A three week process would make it worse. It also suits the people applying. A cook looking for work will not wait while a slow company decides. He takes the first decent offer. Speed here is not laziness. It is the only way to catch good candidates at all.
[] The practical test is the stronger point, and in our view it is the single best thing in the whole system. As section 3.4 explained, watching someone do the real task predicts job performance better than an interview or a certificate. For a job where the skill is making food properly, fast, in heat, under pressure, watching the person do it tells you far more than any conversation about it. Whether PizzaBurg worked this out from theory or just from experience does not matter. It is the right method.
[] Table 5.2  The hiring steps
=== TABLE ===
Step | What happens | Our comment

[] The problem is at the marking stage. A practical test works because it is related to the job. But it only gives the same answer twice if the person watching is marking against a fixed list. Judge by feeling and two managers will pick different people from the same pair of candidates. The same manager will judge more softly on a day when he is short of staff.
[] This is not a small risk here. Policy is set at head office but hiring happens at 22 shops run by managers whose main job is operations. The standard will drift unless something holds it in place. A one page checklist listing the tasks, what to look for and the minimum acceptable level would fix it, and it would cost nothing.

Our view. The hiring method suits the job and suits the pressure the shop is under. Asking a cook to cook is good practice, whether or not anyone at PizzaBurg calls it that. [Heading2] 5.3  Who Leaves
[] On this one Mr. Datta answered in two words. Turnover among office staff is very low. Among floor and kitchen staff it is medium. He gave us no figures and we did not press for any.
[] The gap between the two groups is the interesting part, and it matches what happens in fast food chains everywhere.
[] Table 5.3  Turnover by group
=== TABLE ===
Group | Level | Likely reasons | Effect on the company
Office staff | Very low | Fewer similar jobs nearby. They know the company’s own way of working. A growing chain offers promotion. Leaving costs them more. | Knowledge stays inside. Policy is applied the same way over time.
Floor and kitchen | Medium | Many other employers within walking distance. Hard work at awkward hours. Nothing lost by leaving. Many are young students who leave for exams or after graduating. | Constant hiring. Constant training. Service dips while a new person learns.
=== END TABLE ===
[] Source: our analysis of the interview and of the industry background in Chapter 2.
[] Medium is not a bad result and should not be read as failure. Chapter 2 showed there are hundreds of thousands of food shops in the country, so a cook in Dhaka has options within a short walk. Many staff are young and part time and leave for exams, graduation or a move home. None of that is the employer’s fault. Against that background, medium suggests the welfare and the steady hours are doing something. But nobody counts it. Very low and medium are opinions, not measurements. Without a number the company cannot tell if things are getting worse, cannot see which branch is worst, and cannot tell whether any change it makes has worked. Working out a monthly figure needs nothing the company does not already have in its payroll records.
[] Nobody asks why either. Nothing suggested that anyone finds out why floor staff leave. Turnover looks like a hiring job to be done, not a signal to be read. Yet the difference between losing a weak worker and losing a good one is enormous, and one number hides it. As the next section shows, the two month notice rule hands PizzaBurg an unusually good chance to ask departing workers what went wrong, and that chance is going unused.
[] The turnover picture looks believable and, given the industry, quite respectable. The problem is not the level. It is that turnover is described but never measured, and that leavers are replaced but never asked why they went. Both gaps cost almost nothing to close.
[Heading2] 5.4  The Two Month Notice Rule
[] Every worker must give two months notice before leaving. He told us why, without being asked. It gives the company time to hire a replacement before the person actually goes.
[] Something came out of this that the interview itself never touched on. This is not just a company rule. Section 27 of the Bangladesh Labour Act 2006 says a permanent worker who wants to resign must give 60 days notice in writing. PizzaBurg asks for exactly that. Whether the company copied the law or arrived at two months on its own, the effect is the same. It is in line with the statute, and that is worth pointing out.
[] Then there is what the rule actually does. As section 3.5 said, a notice period does not keep anyone. It turns a sudden hole into a planned one. So its worth comes entirely from what the company does with those 60 days.
[] Figure 5.1  How the notice period could be used
=== TABLE ===
Day 0. Worker gives written notice.
Day 1 to 10. Vacancy confirmed. Hiring starts.
Day 10 to 30. Candidates found. One day interview and practical test.
Day 30 to 50. New worker hired and settled in.
Day 50 to 60. Overlap. The leaver trains the new person.
Day 60. Worker leaves. No gap in the kitchen. Exit interview done.
=== END TABLE ===
[] Source: our own suggestion for using the notice period. The exit interview at day 60 is our recommendation, not current practice.
[] How much the rule is worth changes across the network. In Dhaka the labour market is deep and a nearby branch can lend a cook, so 60 days is generous. In Feni or Noakhali there may be no trained cook available and no branch close enough to help, so 60 days may barely be enough. The same rule carries very different weight depending on where the shop is. That argues for starting the hiring faster at the far outlets.
[] Two chances are being missed. One is the overlap. If hiring starts early, the new person can be working while the old one is still there, and the old one can hand over how that particular kitchen runs. That turns the notice period from an empty buffer into real training, and it costs nothing except starting early. The other is the exit interview. A worker serving out 60 days has already decided to go. He has very little reason to hide why. That is better information than any survey of current staff would give, and PizzaBurg is not collecting it.
[] The two month notice rule is one of the good things in this system. It follows Section 27 of the Labour Act and it protects the company against exactly the thing that hurts a food chain most, a sudden empty station in the kitchen. But the company is only using half of it. Right now the 60 days is a cushion. Used properly the same 60 days would also give a trained replacement and an honest answer about why people leave.
[Heading2] 5.5  What the Workers Get
[Heading3] What he told us
[] Workers who qualify get help with accommodation and food. Health checkups are provided, and that includes maternity care.
[Heading3] What it means
[] Table 5.4  Welfare provided
=== TABLE ===
What is given | What it does for the worker | What it does for the company
Housing help | Removes the biggest single cost of living in a city. Meets a basic need directly. | Adds a lot of real value without adding the same amount to the wage bill. Shorter travel means better attendance. Harder for the worker to walk away.
Food help | Cuts daily spending and guarantees a proper meal during a long shift. | Costs little in a business that already runs a kitchen. Keeps energy up. Visible every single day.
Health checkups | Care that many low paid workers would never pay for themselves. | Catches illness before it becomes absence. Shows the company takes the worker seriously.
Maternity care | Support during pregnancy and after. This is the point where women most often leave a job. | Helps keep women workers. Fits the maternity duties under Sections 45 to 50 of the Labour Act.
=== END TABLE ===
[] Source: our analysis of the interview.
[] These are not small gestures. Rent and food are the two biggest costs a low paid worker in Dhaka carries. Help with both is worth more to that worker than a small pay rise costing the company the same money. Using the ideas from section 3.6, this hits the basic needs first, which is where motivation actually sits at these income levels.
[] But the second idea from section 3.6 gives a warning. Housing and food stop workers being unhappy. They make leaving more expensive. They probably explain why floor turnover is medium rather than high. What they do not do is make anyone work harder. Drive comes from being noticed and promoted. So if PizzaBurg wants to move floor turnover from medium to low, our reading is that more welfare is not the answer. A clear promotion path is. And in a company opening new shops every year, the supervisor jobs to promote people into genuinely exist, which makes such a ladder cheap and believable.
[] The maternity point needs a separate note. Sections 45 to 50 of the Labour Act give a woman worker maternity benefit for eight weeks before and eight weeks after delivery if she has six months of service, and Section 50 protects her against dismissal without good cause during that period. So providing maternity care is both good practice and a legal duty. We were not told whether the full benefit period and the Section 50 protection are written into the company rules. That is worth the company checking.
[] One more thing applies to all four benefits. Mr. Datta said eligible workers, so there are conditions. He did not describe them. When a good benefit has unclear conditions, three things go wrong. People applying for jobs cannot count it, so the company gets no hiring value from it. Current workers may not know they qualify. And when some get it and others do not, it starts to look unfair. A benefit nobody understands delivers only part of what it costs.
=== TABLE ===
Our view. The welfare here is real and it is aimed at the right costs. Along with the practical hiring test, it is one of the two strongest parts of the system. Two things hold it back. The conditions are not explained to anyone, which wastes some of the value. And welfare on its own cannot turn medium turnover into low turnover. That needs a visible way to get promoted.
=== END TABLE ===
[Heading2] 5.6  The Monthly Performance Check
[] Worker performance is checked every month. That was the whole answer, and it is better than we expected. Most companies of this size check once a year. In the restaurant trade, floor staff are often never formally checked at all. Checking monthly means the feedback reaches the worker while he still remembers the shift it refers to. A slide in performance shows up in weeks instead of at the end of a year. And the manager is judging one month he actually watched, not reconstructing twelve from memory.
[] It also fits the work. Kitchen and service performance changes day to day and can be seen all the time. Judging that on a twelve month cycle would be strange. In a business where one bad shift is visible to customers, monthly is about right.
[] Two questions remain, and the interview answered neither.
[] What exactly is being measured? Section 3.5 argued that frequency matters less than whether the things judged are clear, job related and weighted. Nothing suggested a fixed list exists. If the monthly check is really the outlet manager’s general impression, then doing it twelve times a year makes the guesswork more frequent, not more accurate.
[] And is any of it written down? If there is no record, the company cannot compare one month with the next, cannot show a pattern of poor work if a firing is later challenged, and cannot tell whether training helped. That last point connects straight to section 5.8. A file of monthly reviews is exactly the evidence that makes a disciplinary decision stand up under Section 24 of the Labour Act.
[] We should be fair here. Nobody told us that forms and records do not exist. Nobody mentioned them either. Our suggestion in section 9.4 is written with that in mind.
[] The monthly check is one of the best features of the system and it beats what most of the industry does. But its value depends on things we could not see. Without a fixed list of what is being judged and without a written record, a monthly review can turn into a frequent chat that changes nothing and proves nothing.
[Heading2] 5.7  Training
[] Performance measurement is linked with training. Nine words, and it is the cleverest thing in the system. Section 3.5 said the usual failure in training is skipping the first step. Companies buy training because it is available or because a budget must be used, and the training then fixes problems nobody had.
[] By letting the performance check decide who needs training, PizzaBurg has made the appraisal do the job of finding the gap. Put together with a monthly cycle, that creates a short and quick loop.
[] Figure 5.2  The performance and training loop
=== TABLE ===
Monthly performance check
A weakness shows up in one area
Training or correction given
Next month’s check shows whether it worked
and round again
=== END TABLE ===
[] Source: our own drawing of what the manager described.
[] The neat part is that this loop can check itself. Because the review comes round every month, the next review after training is a natural test of whether the training worked. Most companies never get that far. PizzaBurg could, without buying anything.
[] Again there is a condition. You can only see improvement if performance is recorded well enough to compare two months. Without records the loop is an intention rather than a system, and nobody can tell useful training from wasted training.
[] Three other things about training never came up, so we list them as gaps in our information rather than as faults. Nobody described any induction for new joiners, even though the first week is one of the biggest reasons new staff quit early. Nobody described training aimed at the next job rather than the current one, and a growing chain needs to grow its own supervisors. And nobody mentioned training records.
[] Linking training to the performance check is sound thinking and better than the random training common in this industry. With the monthly cycle it creates a fast correction loop that could even prove whether training works. Making that real needs records. As things stand the training looks aimed at fixing today’s weaknesses rather than building tomorrow’s supervisors, which is a problem for a company that keeps opening shops.
[Heading2] 5.8  Rules, Discipline and Firing
[Heading3] What he told us
[] A worker can be dismissed for breaking important company rules. The grounds he named were breaking rules and regulations, sexual harassment, dishonesty and other serious misconduct.
[Heading3] What it means
[] Table 5.5  Grounds for dismissal
=== TABLE ===
Ground | Why the company has it | How it sits with the law
Breaking rules | Standard procedure is how a 22 shop chain keeps food safety, hygiene and service the same everywhere. | Matches the misconduct list in Section 23(4), which covers wilful disobedience and repeated rule breaking.
Sexual harassment | Protects worker safety and dignity. Also a serious risk to the brand if handled badly. | Brings in the 2009 High Court directions, which require every workplace to set up a complaint committee.
Dishonesty | Cash and stock pass through many hands in a food shop. Theft is a known risk in this trade. | Matches theft and fraud against the employer’s property under Section 23(4).
Other serious misconduct | A catch all for bad behaviour not listed above. | Broadly fits Section 23(4), though a catch all only works if the actual behaviour is really misconduct.
=== END TABLE ===
[] Source: our analysis of the interview and of the Bangladesh Labour Act 2006.
[] The grounds themselves are sensible and mostly line up with the law. Naming sexual harassment deserves credit. Plenty of companies in this industry do not list it at all, and putting it on the list says the company treats worker safety as policy rather than as something left to a manager’s mood.
[] But in discipline, how you do it matters as much as what you do. And here we have to talk about what was not said.
[] Nobody described any step by step warning system. Section 3.7 separated serious misconduct, where dismissal after inquiry is right, from smaller problems a worker can fix, where warnings in steps are the accepted way. All four grounds named to us are serious ones. So there may well be a warning system for smaller matters that simply never came up. We are not saying it does not exist. We are saying that in a company of a thousand workers, most discipline problems are lateness, absence, dirty uniform and arguments between staff. None of those should end in dismissal, and all of them need a set procedure.
[] The bigger issue is Section 24. That section is very specific. No punishment under Section 23 is valid unless the charge is written down, the worker gets a copy and at least seven days to reply, he is given a hearing, an inquiry committee with equal numbers from both sides finishes within 60 days, and the employer approves the order. These are not polite extras. A dismissal done without them can be thrown out by the Labour Court no matter how strong the case was. An employer with good grounds and a weak procedure is in worse shape than one with a solid procedure, because the court may never even reach the facts.
[] Then there is harassment. Because there is no separate law, the rules come from the 2009 High Court directions described in section 3.8. Those directions require every workplace, private included, to set up a complaint committee with outside members, to investigate within set times and to keep matters confidential. Nothing in our interview suggested such a committee exists at PizzaBurg. That leaves a strange situation. The company has a rule but no proper place to apply it. If a complaint were made tomorrow, there is no committee to hear it. That hurts both sides. The woman complaining has nowhere proper to go, and the man accused gets no fair process, so whatever is decided can be attacked from either direction. Of everything we found, this is the one we would fix first.
[] One last point of fairness. Mr. Datta said a worker may be dismissed for serious violations. He did not say every violation ends in dismissal, and we are not suggesting he did.
=== TABLE ===
Our view. The reasons PizzaBurg would fire someone are fair, proportionate and mostly in line with the Labour Act, and naming sexual harassment is to the company’s credit. The weakness is in procedure. We saw no sign of a step by step warning system, no sign of the process Section 24 makes compulsory, and no sign of the harassment committee the 2009 court order requires. Good reasons applied through a loose procedure leave the company exposed and leave workers without a reliable way to be treated fairly.
=== END TABLE ===

[Heading2] 6.2  The Two Urgent Ones
[] Most rows are marked check. Those are things nobody asked about, so the company may be fully in line and the answer is an internal look. Two rows are different. In both, PizzaBurg has taken a position that needs a system behind it, and no system was described.
[Heading3] Section 24 and firing
[] PizzaBurg has clear reasons for which a worker can be dismissed. Section 24 says no punishment counts unless the charge is in writing, the worker gets a copy and seven days to answer, he gets a hearing, an inquiry committee with both sides on it finishes within 60 days, and the employer signs off the order.
[] A company that skips those steps is exposed even when it is completely right about the facts. The Labour Court can cancel the dismissal on procedure alone and never look at what the worker did. The fix is cheap. A written procedure, a standard show cause letter, a standing inquiry panel and a file for each case.
[Heading3] The harassment committee
[] By naming sexual harassment as a firing offence, PizzaBurg has taken on the job of deciding such cases. The 2009 High Court order says those decisions must be made by a proper complaint committee with outside members, inside fixed time limits, with confidentiality kept. That order binds private companies until Parliament passes a law.
[] No committee was described to us. So the rule exists with nowhere to apply it. The damage runs both ways. A woman making a complaint has no proper channel. A man accused gets no fair hearing. Whatever gets decided can be challenged from either side. For a brand this visible, with a young workforce and a large social media following, the damage from one badly handled case would go well past the legal side. This is the single most urgent thing in our report.
=== TABLE ===
Our view. On the two points where we have evidence, PizzaBurg is fine. The two month notice matches Section 27 and the maternity care fits Sections 45 to 50. The risk is in procedure, not in intention. No Section 24 process and no harassment committee were described, and those are the two findings here with real legal weight. Both can be fixed for almost nothing.
=== END TABLE ===
[Heading2] 6.3  Compared With the Rest of the Industry
[] Against the local trade, several things stand out. Formal performance checks for floor staff are rare in this industry and monthly checks rarer still. Housing and food help go past what a small operator gives. Having a General Manager of Human Resource at head office is itself a sign of a more serious company, since many chains this size run people matters through operations or accounts. And a two month notice rule that is actually enforced is more than many operators bother with.
[] Against the big foreign chains the picture flips. They arrive with written service rules, set training levels, complaint procedures and promotion ladders as part of the franchise package, and PizzaBurg is fighting them for the same cooks. Its advantages are welfare, steady guaranteed hours and faster promotion in a growing company. Its disadvantage is the missing paperwork that tells an experienced worker the job is secure and going somewhere. That is a business reason for writing things down, separate from the legal reason.

[] Chapter Nine
[Heading1] What We Suggest
[] Each suggestion below comes out of something we actually found. We have kept them to things a company this size can afford and can start soon. They are listed in the order we would do them.
[] 9.1  Set up a harassment committee and write down the firing procedure
[] These go together because they are the same problem. The company has taken positions it has no machinery to carry out. It names harassment as a firing offence with no committee to hear a complaint, and it has grounds for dismissal with no procedure matching Section 24. Both leave it open to losing a case it should win, and the harassment one carries damage no court order can measure.
[] What to do. Set up a standing complaint committee for harassment, headed by a woman if possible, with outside members as the 2009 order requires, and write down how a complaint is made, how long the inquiry takes and what stays confidential. Separately, write a disciplinary procedure with the Section 24 steps in it: a written charge, at least seven days to reply, a hearing, an inquiry panel with both sides on it finishing inside 60 days, and formal approval of the order. Give both to every outlet manager and put a short version on the notice board in each shop. Have a labour lawyer read them before they are issued.
[] 9.2  Check the staffing numbers twice a year
[] We are not asking the company to drop the fixed staffing rule. It works and should stay. We are asking it to test the number. A headcount set on opening day only stays right while the shop stays the same, and the gap stays invisible until service breaks or money is wasted.
[] What to do. Twice a year, compare each shop’s fixed number against simple things the business already tracks. Number of orders, share of delivery, how long queues get at peak, and overtime hours. Where a shop has clearly moved, change its number. Leave it alone in between so the company keeps the benefit of a steady team.
[] 9.3  Make a marking sheet for the practical test
[] The practical test is the strongest part of the hiring system and nothing about it needs to change. What it needs is a sheet. Without a fixed list to mark against, two managers will pick different people from the same pair of candidates.
[] What to do. Make a one page checklist. List the tasks the candidate must do, what to look for in each, a simple score out of five, and the lowest total that still gets a job offer. Keep the filled sheet with the appointment file. That also gives the company a record if a hiring decision is ever questioned.
[] 9.4  Use a proper monthly review form
[] Monthly checking is a strength and the frequency should stay. What it needs is something to check against and somewhere to write the result. A frequent check against a general impression is still a general impression, and with no record nothing can be compared or proved later.
[] What to do. Introduce a one page monthly form for outlet staff. Score a small number of things that can actually be seen and that matter to the job: attendance and lateness, food quality, speed and order accuracy, hygiene, how the worker deals with customers, and teamwork. Give the important ones more weight. Ask the manager to write one line on the best and worst item. Have the worker sign to confirm the feedback was given. Send the form to head office.
[] 9.5  Count turnover every month
[] You cannot manage what you do not measure. Very low and medium cannot show a trend or point at the worst branch. The numbers needed are already sitting in the payroll records.
[] What to do. Each month, divide the number of leavers by the average number of workers. Do it separately for each shop and for each group of staff. Keep a rolling twelve month line so the trend is visible. Separate people who quit from people who were let go, and use their last review score to see whether the company lost a good worker or a weak one. Set a target for floor staff and hold branches against it.
[] 9.6  Use the notice period properly
[] The two month rule already hands the company the most valuable thing here, which is time. Right now it is only a cushion. Used properly the same 60 days gives a trained replacement in the kitchen before the old worker walks out, and an honest answer about why he is going.
[] What to do. Start the replacement process the day notice is given. Confirm the vacancy and begin looking within ten days. Finish hiring by day thirty. Have the new person in and settled by day fifty. Keep the last ten days for handover, with the leaver showing the new person how that kitchen runs. Move faster at the district town shops where replacements are harder to find. In the final week, have someone from head office rather than the worker’s own manager ask him why he is leaving, and gather the answers every three months.
[] 9.7  Explain the welfare rules and build a promotion ladder
[] A benefit with unclear conditions delivers only part of what it costs. Applicants cannot count it and workers may not know they qualify. Section 5.5 also argued that housing and food stop people being unhappy but do not make them ambitious. A visible way up is the stronger lever, and a chain opening shops has the supervisor jobs to make it real.
[] What to do. Write a one page sheet listing each benefit, who qualifies and how to ask for it. Give it to every worker on joining and pin it up in each shop. Separately, set out four steps from trainee to kitchen staff to senior kitchen staff to shift supervisor, with the skills, the minimum time served and the pay difference for each. When a new shop needs a supervisor, promote from inside.
[] 9.8  Add induction and training for the next job up
[] The current arrangement fixes weaknesses that already exist. It does not build what the company will need next. A chain that keeps opening shops needs supervisors, and those are cheaper grown inside. Nobody described any induction either, though the first week is one of the strongest reasons new staff quit early.
[] What to do. Give every new worker an induction in the first three days covering the job, food safety and hygiene, service standards, what welfare they get, the rules that can get them fired and how to make a complaint. Have them sign a short checklist. Separately, run a short supervisor course for workers the monthly review picks out as ready. Keep a simple training record for each worker so the reviews before and after training can be compared.
[] 9.9  Keep central records
[] Nearly every suggestion above needs records. Review forms have to be filed to be compared, turnover worked out from headcount, discipline written down to hold up. Across 22 shops, notes kept locally cannot do any of this. This is the one that makes the others possible.
[] What to do. Keep one central record for each worker showing the appointment letter and ID card issued under Section 5, what welfare they qualify for, monthly review results, training done, any disciplinary action, and how and when they left. A cheap HR or payroll package sold in Bangladesh would be enough. A properly controlled shared spreadsheet kept by head office would do to start with.
[] 9.10  Do an internal check against the Labour Act
[] Table 6.1 lists several sections nobody asked about, so we do not know where the company stands. Appointment letters and ID cards under Section 5. Firing without cause under Section 26. The full maternity entitlement. Working hours and overtime. The company may be fully in line on all of them. The point here is to know, and to be able to show it.
[] What to do. Have someone go through the company’s employment practice against the Labour Act 2006 and the Labour Rules 2015, covering at least the sections in Table 6.1, and get a labour lawyer to confirm the result. Repeat it once a year and after any change in the law.

[] Chapter Ten
[Heading1] Conclusion
[] We set out to see how PizzaBurg manages its people. The company started with one shop in Mirpur in January 2018 and now runs 22 outlets with close to a thousand workers. Our information came from a face to face meeting with Mr. Ranian Datta, the General Manager of Human Resource, on 26 August 2026, which we then compared with what we studied in class and with the Bangladesh Labour Act 2006.
[] The main thing we found is that this system has one purpose running through all of it. Keep the shops open and running. Every rule the manager described serves that. The fixed number of workers makes cost predictable. One day hiring keeps a station from staying empty. The practical test makes sure the person hired can actually do the work. Two months notice turns a sudden loss into a planned one. Housing and food keep workers turning up. Monthly checks catch a problem in weeks instead of months. It holds together, and it fits a chain that is opening shops fast.
[] The weak points all look the same. In every area we studied, the method was sound and the paperwork was missing. A good hiring test with no marking sheet. Frequent reviews with no fixed measures and no records. Turnover talked about but never counted. Welfare given but never explained. Firing grounds with no procedure attached. This is what happens when a business grows faster than its office.
[] Two of those gaps go beyond untidiness. Without a procedure matching Section 24, a dismissal can be cancelled on process alone and the facts may never be heard. Without a harassment complaint committee, the company has a rule with nowhere to apply it, and both the person complaining and the person accused are left without a fair route. Those two are our most serious findings, and they are also two of the cheapest things to fix.
[] PizzaBurg is at a point many successful Bangladeshi companies reach. A business the owner watches himself can run on judgement and on knowing everyone. With a thousand workers in ten towns, judgement has to be written into forms or it stops being the same judgement, and knowing everyone has to become records or it disappears. The job now is to build the paperwork without losing the speed.
[] One last word on what this report is and is not. We spoke to one person, once. We did not see a single company document and we did not speak to a single ordinary worker. So where we have written that something was not evidenced, we mean nobody told us about it. It may well exist. Inside those limits, our conclusion is that PizzaBurg has built a working and sensible people system that carried it through very fast growth, and that its next job is to write that system down before growing any further.
